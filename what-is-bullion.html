Bullion Explained: A Practical Guide for U.S. Investors

Bullion is physical precious metal — gold, silver, platinum, or palladium — refined to investment-grade purity and held in bars, ingots, or coins primarily for its metal content rather than its face value or collectibility. For U.S. investors, it functions as a tangible, counterparty-free asset that can hedge against inflation and currency risk, though you pay a premium over spot price to acquire it and earn no yield while holding it.

A few quick facts to orient you:

  • The London Bullion Market Association (LBMA) sets the global benchmark for gold and silver bar quality, requiring .9999 fineness for gold and .999 for silver to qualify as “Good Delivery.”
  • The U.S. Mint produces the American Gold Eagle and American Silver Eagle, two of the most widely recognized and liquid bullion coins available to U.S. buyers.
  • Investopedia defines bullion as physical gold and silver of high purity — often 99.5% or higher — used as an investment hedge.

Key Takeaways

Bullion’s value as a portfolio asset comes from its purity, physical form, and freedom from counterparty obligations — but only when sourced from verified supply chains and held in properly structured custody.

What actually counts as bullion?

Not every piece of precious metal qualifies. The defining characteristic is that the metal’s value comes almost entirely from its refined purity and weight, not from artistry, rarity as a collectible, or legal tender status.

Qualifying metals include gold, silver, platinum, and palladium. Gold and silver dominate the retail market. Platinum and palladium are less common but trade actively in industrial and investment channels.

Physical forms break into three main categories, each with different liquidity and cost profiles:

  • Bars and ingots: Cast bars are poured into molds and tend to carry lower premiums. Minted bars are stamped from flat metal sheets, have a cleaner finish, and usually cost slightly more. Both come in sizes from 1 gram to 400 troy ounces (the standard London Good Delivery bar).
  • Government-minted bullion coins: Struck by sovereign mints, these carry a nominal face value but trade at their metal content. The American Gold Eagle (U.S. Mint) and American Silver Eagle (U.S. Mint) are the most recognized examples for U.S. buyers. Retail bullion comes in three main forms — bars, coins, and rounds — each with distinct premium and resale characteristics.
  • Privately minted rounds: Look like coins but carry no government guarantee or face value. They typically offer lower premiums than sovereign coins but are harder to resell quickly because dealers and buyers scrutinize them more carefully.
  • Other forms: Wafers, granules, and grain are used mainly in industrial and refining contexts, though some investors hold wafers for compact storage.

Bullion vs. numismatic coins: A numismatic coin’s price is driven by rarity, condition, and collector demand, often far above its metal content. A bullion coin’s price tracks spot metal prices closely. The American Gold Eagle straddles both worlds — it has some numismatic appeal — but its primary market is bullion buyers. Jewelry is excluded from the bullion category because fabrication costs, design, and retail markup push its price well above melt value, making it inefficient as a pure metal holding.

How is bullion purity and weight measured?

Purity is expressed as fineness — a decimal or parts-per-thousand figure representing the proportion of pure metal. A bar stamped .925 is sterling silver, which most dealers do not classify as investment bullion.

Common purity thresholds for investment-grade bullion:

Product type Typical fineness Common example
Government bullion coin ranges depending on type American Gold Eagle (.9167 Au)
Cast bar very high fineness Generic 1 oz gold bar
Minted bar very high fineness PAMP Suisse, Perth Mint
Private round high fineness Generic silver round

The American Gold Eagle is an interesting outlier: it contains exactly 1 troy ounce of gold but is alloyed to .9167 fineness for durability. Its gold content is guaranteed by the U.S. Mint, so the lower fineness does not reduce its investment value.

Weight units matter because precious metals use the troy ounce (31.1035 grams), not the avoirdupois ounce (28.35 grams) used for everyday goods. Common sizes run from gram and fractional ounces up to large bars including 1 oz, 10 oz, kilo, and 100 oz sizes.

Verification methods range from simple to professional:

  • Weight and dimensions check: Every legitimate product has published specifications. A digital scale accurate to 0.01 grams and a caliper catch most counterfeits immediately.
  • Magnet test: Gold, silver, platinum, and palladium are non-magnetic. A strong rare-earth magnet sliding across the surface should not stick or slow noticeably.
  • Visual hallmark inspection: Look for fineness stamps, mint marks, and serial numbers. Tamper-evident assay cards and sealed packaging from reputable refiners add another layer.
  • XRF (X-ray fluorescence) testing: A non-destructive method that reads elemental composition to several decimal places. Most reputable dealers own an XRF gun; you can request a test on high-value purchases.
  • Fire assay: The oldest and most precise method, dissolving a sample in acid to isolate pure metal. Used for large commercial transactions and LBMA Good Delivery verification, not routine retail purchases.

Pro Tip: For purchases under $5,000, a calibrated scale and magnet test catch the vast majority of fakes. Reserve professional XRF or assay for purchases above that threshold, for large cast bars from unfamiliar sources, or any time a deal looks unusually cheap.

How is bullion priced, and what drives liquidity?

The foundation is the spot price — the current market price for immediate delivery of one troy ounce of a given metal. APMEX publishes live gold spot prices continuously, and the LBMA sets twice-daily benchmark fixes used by institutions worldwide. Spot prices reflect global supply and demand, currency movements, interest rates, and macro sentiment.

What you actually pay at retail is spot plus a dealer premium. A simplified example:

Component Illustrative amount
Gold spot price $X per troy oz
Dealer premium (1 oz coin) $X plus a moderate premium
Shipping and insurance $X plus a small premium
Retail price you pay Spot + all above

Premiums vary by form, size, and market conditions. Smaller fractional coins usually carry higher premiums, while large cast bars tend to have lower premiums. Government coins from the U.S. Mint typically sit in the middle but command higher premiums than private rounds because of their global recognition and guaranteed purity.

What drives premiums higher:

  • Small size or fractional weight
  • High-demand products with limited supply (e.g., American Silver Eagle during supply crunches)
  • Fancy packaging or special editions
  • Tight physical supply relative to paper market demand

Liquidity — how easily you can sell — follows recognition. Sovereign coins like the American Gold Eagle and American Silver Eagle are accepted by virtually every dealer worldwide without question. Large cast bars from lesser-known refiners may require a dealer to run an XRF test before buying, which slows the transaction and can reduce the price offered. Private rounds occupy the lowest liquidity tier; some dealers won’t buy them at all.

Quick rules for better pricing:

  • Buy 1 oz or larger to minimize per-ounce premium.
  • Stick to sovereign coins or LBMA-accredited refiner bars for easiest resale.
  • Compare at least three dealer quotes before buying; live spot price feeds make this straightforward.
  • Check the dealer’s published buyback price before you purchase, not after.

Common bullion products U.S. investors buy

American Gold Eagle (U.S. Mint): The flagship U.S. gold bullion coin, available in 1 oz, 1/2 oz, 1/4 oz, and 1/10 oz. Its .9167 fineness is lower than some competitors, but the U.S. government guarantees its gold content, making it one of the most liquid gold products on earth. It is also eligible for gold IRAs.

American Silver Eagle (U.S. Mint): The best-selling silver bullion coin in the world by most measures. Struck in .999 fine silver at 1 troy ounce, it carries a $1 face value and is IRA-eligible. Premiums run higher than generic silver rounds, but the resale market is deep and immediate.

Gold and silver bars: Minted bars from LBMA-accredited refiners (such as PAMP Suisse or Valcambi) come with assay cards and serial numbers, making them straightforward to authenticate and resell. Cast bars cost less per ounce but require more scrutiny on resale. Kilo bars and 100 oz silver bars appeal to buyers prioritizing low premiums over portability.

Private rounds: Cheaper to buy than sovereign coins, but the tradeoff is real. Dealers discount them on buyback, and some won’t touch them without testing.

IRA eligibility requires specific purity thresholds: gold must be .995 or finer (the American Gold Eagle is a statutory exception), silver must be .999, and platinum and palladium must be .9995. The metal must also be held by an IRS-approved custodian, not at home.

Attributes to verify before buying:

  • Fineness stamp and weight match published specifications
  • Serial number and assay card present (for bars)
  • Tamper-evident packaging intact
  • Dealer’s buyback policy in writing
  • Shipping insured to full replacement value

Why do investors hold bullion?

The core rationale is what bullion is not: it is not a liability of any bank, government, or corporation; understanding proper structures for gold custody and tokenization is crucial for modern investors (Gold & Commodity Tokenisation in UAE: VARA Legal Framework, Licensing & Compliance Guide). When you own a gold bar outright, there is no counterparty who can default on it. That property makes it structurally different from stocks, bonds, ETFs, or bank deposits.

Main reasons investors allocate to bullion:

  • Inflation hedge: Gold has historically preserved purchasing power over long periods. When currency loses value, the metal price tends to rise in nominal terms.
  • Safe-haven demand: During financial crises, sovereign debt stress, or geopolitical shocks, capital often flows into physical metals. Academic research suggests bullion functions as a low-correlation asset during some crisis periods, though short-term performance against other assets can be volatile.
  • Portfolio diversification: Gold’s correlation with equities and bonds is generally low, which means adding a modest allocation can reduce overall portfolio volatility without necessarily reducing expected return.
  • Counterparty risk reduction: Physical bullion held in allocated storage is yours outright. Gold ETFs and futures contracts involve intermediaries; physical metal does not.

Quick comparison with other asset classes:

  • Stocks: Higher long-term growth potential, generate dividends, but carry company and market risk. Bullion generates no income.
  • Bonds: Provide income and lower volatility than equities, but are exposed to inflation and credit risk. Bullion has no credit risk.
  • Real estate: Generates income and appreciates over time but is illiquid and management-intensive. Bullion is highly liquid but yields nothing.

Pro Tip: Many institutional allocation frameworks suggest a moderate weighting in physical precious metals as part of portfolio diversification. Combining physical bullion with a smaller paper exposure (a gold ETF) gives you the counterparty-free anchor plus the liquidity of a tradeable security — two different tools for two different purposes.

How to buy, store, and sell bullion in the United States

Buying checklist

  1. Verify dealer reputation. Look for membership in the Industry Council for Tangible Assets (ICTA) or the American Numismatic Association (ANA), verified customer reviews, and a published buyback policy. Review gold buying strategies before committing to a dealer.
  2. Compare premiums across at least three dealers. Use a live spot price feed as your baseline.
  3. Confirm payment, shipping, and insurance terms. Reputable dealers ship fully insured; never accept uninsured delivery on a high-value order.
  4. Check buyback terms in writing before you buy, not after.
  5. Confirm IRA eligibility if the purchase is for a self-directed IRA — purity and custodian requirements apply.

Storage options

Home storage gives you immediate access and no ongoing fees, but requires a quality safe (bolted to the structure, fire-rated), a homeowner’s or renter’s insurance rider specifically covering precious metals, and discretion about who knows what you hold.

Third-party allocated vaults and insured depositories (such as Brink’s, Delaware Depository, or Loomis) hold your metal in your name, segregated from other clients’ holdings. This is the standard for IRA-held bullion and for larger institutional positions. For a deeper look at institutional custody practices, Galamigold’s physical gold trading guide covers the key considerations.

Unallocated storage means the vault holds a pool of metal and owes you a quantity, not specific bars. It is cheaper but reintroduces counterparty risk — the opposite of why many investors hold physical metal in the first place.

Selling routes

  • Dealer buyback: Fastest and most straightforward. Expect to receive spot minus a small spread. Dealers with published buyback prices are preferable.
  • Online secondary markets: Platforms like eBay allow private sales at closer to retail, but require shipping, insurance, and buyer verification.
  • Private sale: Highest potential price but slowest and requires the buyer to trust your authentication or pay for independent testing.

U.S. tax summary

The IRS classifies physical precious metals as collectibles. Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income. State sales tax on bullion purchases varies by state; many states exempt investment-grade bullion, but rules differ. Consult a qualified tax professional for your specific situation — IRS guidance on collectibles is the authoritative starting point, and gold trading compliance considerations are worth reviewing for larger transactions.

Pro Tip: Keep meticulous purchase records — date, price paid, dealer, and product description. The IRS requires you to report gains, and your cost basis documentation is your only protection against overpaying on taxes when you sell.

How to spot counterfeits and common bullion scams

Most fakes target popular, high-value products: 1 oz gold bars, American Gold Eagles, and American Silver Eagles. The counterfeiting has grown more sophisticated, which makes a layered verification approach worth the five minutes it takes.

Practical authentication checks:

  • Weight and dimensions first. A 1 oz gold coin should weigh exactly 31.1035 grams (or the published specification for that product). Even a 0.1-gram discrepancy is a red flag.
  • Magnet test. Genuine gold, silver, platinum, and palladium are non-magnetic. Tungsten-filled gold bars (a known counterfeit method) will also pass the magnet test, which is why weight and XRF are still necessary for bars.
  • Visual inspection. Check edge reeding on coins, font consistency on hallmarks, and the depth of relief on minted designs. Counterfeits often show soft details or inconsistent lettering.
  • Assay card and packaging. Tamper-evident sealed packaging from LBMA-accredited refiners is difficult to replicate convincingly. Broken seals or mismatched serial numbers are immediate disqualifiers.
  • XRF testing for high-value purchases. Request it from the dealer or take the piece to a local coin dealer with the equipment.

Common scams to know:

  • Inflated buyback promises: A seller guarantees you’ll be able to resell at a specific price. No legitimate dealer makes that promise; metal prices move.
  • Bogus certificates of authenticity: A certificate is only as good as the issuer. A certificate from an unknown “assay company” means nothing. Stick to products from LBMA-accredited refiners or sovereign mints.
  • Unverifiable private rounds: Rounds with no mint name, no serial number, and no assay card are nearly impossible to authenticate quickly. Avoid them unless you have XRF access.
  • Informal sellers without verification: Social media and classified ad sellers often have no accountability. If you cannot verify the seller’s identity and the product’s provenance, walk away.

The single highest-risk action in bullion buying is paying for metal you cannot immediately verify. Never rely solely on a certificate, a photo, or a seller’s assurance. Weight, dimensions, and a magnet test take under two minutes and catch most fakes before money changes hands.

Galami’s perspective on bullion as a portfolio asset

Physical bullion occupies a specific and often misunderstood role in serious portfolio construction. The conversation around gold tends to swing between two extremes: either it is treated as a crisis-only panic buy, or dismissed as a yield-free relic. Both framings miss the point.

What bullion actually offers is structural independence from the financial system’s counterparty web. Every stock, bond, ETF, and bank deposit involves a chain of obligations. Physical metal held in allocated custody does not. That property is not exciting in a bull market, but it is exactly what you want when the chain breaks.
