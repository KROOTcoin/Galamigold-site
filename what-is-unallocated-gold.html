Unallocated Gold Explained: Ownership, Risks, and When to Use It

Unallocated gold is a contractual claim on a specified quantity of metal held by an intermediary, not legal title to any specific, identifiable bar. You are a creditor, not an owner. The LBMA confirms that the vast majority of wholesale precious-metal trades clear over unallocated accounts precisely because of their speed and low friction. The IMF classifies unallocated balances as deposit-like financial assets, not monetary gold, which has direct implications for insolvency treatment and reserve accounting.

The practical recommendation: use unallocated accounts for short-term trading, liquidity management, or wholesale settlement where transaction speed matters. Choose allocated gold when physical ownership, long-term capital preservation, or delivery certainty is the priority. What does unallocated gold mean, exactly?

Unallocated gold works like a bank account denominated in metal rather than dollars. When you buy gold through an unallocated account, the provider credits your account with a quantity, say 10 troy ounces, but those ounces are not set aside in a vault with your name on them. They sit in a pooled reserve alongside the claims of every other account holder. The provider manages that pool, and you hold a contractual right to receive the equivalent quantity of metal on demand, subject to the terms of your agreement.

The legal distinction matters enormously. As the World Gold Council’s glossary puts it, unallocated gold is a claim on gold recorded as a balance with an intermediary rather than specific bars, and that model introduces counterparty risk. In legal terms, you are an unsecured creditor of the provider, not a bailee holding title to physical property. If the provider enters insolvency, your claim ranks alongside other unsecured creditors, not ahead of them.

Key mechanics of unallocated accounts

  • Credit/debit model: Purchases and sales are recorded as account entries. No physical movement occurs for most transactions.
  • Pooled reserve: The provider maintains a pool of metal to back aggregate claims, but the ratio of physical metal to outstanding claims varies by provider and is rarely disclosed in full.
  • Fractional quantities: You can buy 0.5 ounces or 3.7 ounces without needing a whole London Good Delivery bar (which runs roughly 400 troy ounces).
  • Settlement convenience: Trades settle quickly because no physical allocation or bar identification is required.

Claim vs. title: A claim gives you the right to demand delivery of a quantity of metal. Title gives you ownership of specific property. Unallocated gold is a claim. Allocated gold confers title through a bailment arrangement, where the provider holds your specific bars as custodian.

Practical example: You wire $50,000 to an unallocated gold account. The provider credits your account with the equivalent quantity at the spot price. You now have a contractual right to that quantity. If you want physical delivery, you must first request conversion to allocated metal, pay any associated fees, and wait for the provider to identify and segregate specific bars. Until that conversion happens, you own a claim, not gold.

How does unallocated gold compare to allocated gold?

The two models differ on every dimension that matters to an investor: legal status, delivery rights, cost structure, liquidity, and risk profile.

Dimension Unallocated gold Allocated gold
Legal/ownership status Unsecured creditor claim on a quantity of metal Legal title to specific, identified bars held in bailment
Counterparty risk High: provider insolvency puts your claim at risk Low: your bars are segregated and not part of provider’s estate
Physical delivery Possible, but requires conversion and fees; not guaranteed immediately Straightforward: bars are already identified and segregated
Storage/insurance costs Typically low or zero (pooled, provider-absorbed) Higher: you pay for segregated vault space and insurance
Spreads and trading fees Tighter spreads; lower transaction friction Wider spreads; allocation/deallocation fees apply
Liquidity High: fast settlement, fractional quantities, no bar identification needed Lower: physical handling and paperwork slow transactions
Suitability Short-term traders, wholesale dealers, liquidity-focused institutions Long-term holders, capital preservation, physical-delivery mandates

The most consequential trade-off is counterparty risk versus cost. Allocated gold costs more to hold because you are paying for genuine segregation: a specific vault location, a weight list, bar serial numbers, and insurance tied to your property. As the Royal Mint explains, allocated gold is earmarked and owned outright, while unallocated gold is effectively on loan to the institution, exposing the buyer to insolvency risk while typically incurring lower short-term fees.

For a trader executing dozens of transactions per month, those lower fees and faster settlement times justify the counterparty exposure. For a family office holding gold as a multi-year reserve asset, paying storage and insurance for allocated bars is the cost of sleeping well.

Who typically prefers unallocated:

  • Wholesale dealers and market makers
  • Institutions using gold for short-term liquidity or collateral
  • Investors making frequent fractional trades

Who typically prefers allocated:

  • Long-term investors prioritizing capital preservation
  • Institutions with physical-delivery mandates
  • Anyone whose investment thesis depends on owning the metal, not a claim on it

Pro Tip: If you hold an unallocated position that has grown beyond what you originally intended as a trading float, that is usually the signal to convert at least a portion to allocated. The conversion cost is a one-time expense; the counterparty risk you eliminate is ongoing.

How unallocated gold is used in wholesale markets

The scale of unallocated gold in professional markets is striking. According to the LBMA, a very large majority of precious metals traded on the interbank, wholesale, and OTC market clear over unallocated Loco London accounts. That figure reflects a structural reality: physical gold is heavy, expensive to move, and slow to verify. Unallocated accounts eliminate most of that friction.

The LBMA estimates that a very large majority of precious metals traded on the interbank/wholesale/OTC market clear over unallocated Loco London accounts, making unallocated the operational backbone of global gold trading.

Common market applications

  • OTC spot settlement: When a dealer sells gold to a bank, both parties credit and debit unallocated accounts. No bars move. The trade settles in hours, not days.
  • Gold swaps: A central bank or institution temporarily exchanges unallocated gold claims for dollars (or another currency), agreeing to reverse the transaction at a set date. Because no physical movement is required, swaps are operationally cheap.
  • Gold loans: A mining company borrows unallocated gold from a bank, sells it for cash to fund operations, and repays the loan in metal later. The entire transaction runs through unallocated accounts.
  • Interbank clearing: Banks net their gold positions against each other daily through unallocated account entries, settling only the net difference rather than gross physical flows.

Liquidity is the engine here. As Investopedia notes, liquidity refers to how quickly and cheaply an asset can be converted to cash or traded without moving its price. Unallocated gold is structurally more liquid than allocated metal because it requires no physical handling, no bar identification, and no vault coordination. That liquidity premium is why institutional products, including certain gold ETFs tracked on platforms like ETFdb, interact with unallocated markets to manage their underlying exposure efficiently.

For institutional traders, the operational speed difference is material. An unallocated trade can settle same-day or next-day. Converting that same position to allocated metal, identifying specific bars, and arranging custody transfer can take several business days and involves additional paperwork under LBMA Good Delivery standards.

What are the real risks of unallocated gold?

The primary risk is straightforward: if your provider fails, you are an unsecured creditor. You do not have a claim on specific bars sitting in a vault. You have a claim in the insolvency proceedings, ranked alongside other general creditors, which typically means recovering cents on the dollar after secured creditors are paid.

Under most legal frameworks, unallocated gold account holders rank as unsecured creditors in insolvency. Their claim is on a quantity of metal, not on specific property, which means they do not benefit from the legal protections that apply to bailment arrangements or segregated custody.

Risk breakdown

  • Counterparty/credit risk: The provider’s financial health directly determines whether your claim is honored. A solvent provider with strong reserves is very different from one running a fractionally backed pool under stress.
  • Rehypothecation: Some providers lend or pledge the metal backing your claim to third parties. If those arrangements fail, the pool backing your account may be smaller than the aggregate claims against it.
  • Pooling opacity: Providers are not always required to disclose the exact ratio of physical metal to outstanding claims. You may not know how well-backed your position actually is.
  • Limitation clauses: Provider contracts often include force majeure or market disruption clauses that can delay or restrict delivery, even when you have a valid claim.
  • Operational settlement errors: In high-volume clearing, account crediting errors occur. Without regular statement reconciliation, discrepancies can go undetected.

The IMF’s analysis treats unallocated balances as deposit-like financial assets in many statistical frameworks, which is precisely the point: they behave more like a bank deposit than a property right. A bank deposit is insured up to FDIC limits in the U.S. An unallocated gold account typically is not. That gap in protection is the risk most retail-oriented marketing materials understate.

The CoinMarketCap Academy entry on allocated gold notes that pooled inventory enables fractional quantities and faster settlement but does not guarantee assignment of specific bars unless the position is converted to allocated metal. That is not a minor caveat. It is the defining structural limitation of the unallocated model.

What do unallocated and allocated gold cost to hold in the U.S.?

Cost structures differ significantly between the two models, and the gap widens with holding period.

Typical fee categories

Unallocated accounts:

  • Provider spread (bid/ask) on entry and exit, often 0.1%–0.5% per transaction
  • Maintenance or account fees (some providers charge monthly; others absorb costs in the spread)
  • Delivery fees if you request physical conversion
  • No separate storage or insurance line item (pooled costs are embedded in the spread)

Allocated accounts:

  • Provider spread, typically wider than unallocated
  • Segregated storage fees, usually charged annually as a percentage of metal value
  • Insurance fees, either bundled with storage or charged separately
  • Allocation/deallocation fees when converting between account types
  • Assay and packaging fees if bars are moved or delivered

For a short holding period, say under six months, unallocated is almost always cheaper on a total-cost basis. The spread is the main cost, and you pay it once in and once out.

Pro Tip: *To estimate total holding cost, add the round-trip spread (entry + exit) to annualized storage and insurance fees multiplied by your expected holding period in years.

U.S. tax considerations

In the U.S., gains from selling gold, whether held in allocated or unallocated accounts, are generally subject to capital gains tax. However, the specific treatment depends on the structure of the account, the type of entity holding it, and whether delivery has occurred.

A conversion from unallocated to allocated metal, or a request for physical delivery, may constitute a taxable event depending on how the transaction is structured. Recordkeeping is critical: maintain statements showing acquisition cost, date, quantity, and any conversion or delivery events. Consult a U.S. tax professional before executing large conversions or taking delivery, as the rules are fact-specific and the IRS has not issued comprehensive guidance covering every account structure.

How do you decide between unallocated and allocated gold?

The decision comes down to five variables. Work through them in order.

Decision checklist:

  1. Investment horizon: Holding for less than a year? Unallocated’s lower carrying cost usually wins. Holding for multiple years? Allocated’s counterparty-risk elimination justifies the storage fees.
  2. Need for physical possession: If your mandate, trust document, or personal preference requires owning specific bars, allocated is the only option. Unallocated cannot guarantee delivery of specific metal.
  3. Counterparty risk tolerance: Are you comfortable being an unsecured creditor of your provider? If not, allocated is the answer regardless of cost.
  4. Transaction frequency: Frequent traders benefit from unallocated’s tighter spreads and faster settlement. Infrequent, long-term holders rarely need that liquidity.
  5. Cost sensitivity: Model the total cost over your expected holding period using the framework above.

Questions to ask any provider

  • What percentage of your aggregate client claims is backed by physical metal at any given time?
  • Are client assets held in bailment (allocated) or as a general creditor claim (unallocated)?
  • How often are holdings independently audited, and can I see the most recent report?
  • What insurance covers client metal, and does it cover my specific position or only the pool?
  • What happens to my claim if you enter administration or insolvency?
  • What is the minimum quantity for physical delivery, and what is the full cost breakdown?
  • Will you put your custody and segregation policies in writing as part of the account agreement?

Red flags

  • Audit reports that are vague, infrequent, or not independently verified
  • Inconsistent answers about physical backing ratios
  • No clear delivery process or delivery minimums that effectively prevent most clients from ever taking physical metal
  • Contracts with broad force majeure clauses that could suspend delivery indefinitely
  • Unwillingness to provide written terms on segregation, insurance, or insolvency treatment

Who should prefer unallocated: Active traders, wholesale market participants, institutions managing short-term liquidity, and anyone using gold as collateral in swap or lending arrangements.

Who should prefer allocated: Long-term investors, family offices, pension funds with physical-delivery mandates, and anyone for whom the counterparty risk of an unsecured creditor position is unacceptable.

How do you convert unallocated gold to allocated or take physical delivery?

Converting an unallocated position to allocated metal, or requesting physical delivery, follows a predictable sequence. The timeline and costs vary by provider and position size, but the steps are consistent.

Step-by-step conversion process

  1. Submit a conversion or delivery request in writing to your provider, specifying the quantity and preferred form (allocated account credit or physical shipment).
  2. Provider confirms availability and quotes the applicable fees: allocation fee, assay/packaging if required, shipping, and insurance.
  3. Bars are identified and segregated. The provider assigns specific bars from inventory, records serial numbers and weights, and issues a weight list. Under LBMA Good Delivery standards, each bar carries a unique serial number, refiner mark, fineness, and gross weight.
  4. Custody transfer or shipment is arranged. For allocated account credit, the provider updates your account record with bar details. For physical delivery, the provider arranges insured shipment to your designated address or vault.
  5. You confirm receipt and verify the weight list. Match bar serial numbers against the weight list provided. For large positions, request an independent assay.

Typical timelines

  • Conversion to allocated account: 1–3 business days for standard positions; up to 5 business days for large or complex transfers.
  • Physical delivery (domestic U.S.): 3–7 business days after confirmation, depending on vault location and logistics provider.
  • International delivery: 7–21 business days, subject to customs clearance and export documentation.

Likely cost items

  • Allocation fee: varies by provider, often a flat fee per bar or a percentage of metal value
  • Assay and packaging: charged per bar when bars are repackaged or re-assayed
  • Shipping and insurance: typically 0.1%–0.3% of metal value for insured domestic shipment
  • Sales tax: in most U.S. states, physical delivery of gold bullion is exempt from sales tax, but rules vary by state and transaction structure. Verify with a tax advisor before taking delivery.

Practical tip: Before any transfer, request the full weight list with bar serial numbers and verify them against an independent audit if the position exceeds a few hundred ounces. Discrepancies are rare but not impossible, and they are far easier to resolve before custody changes hands.

What do the LBMA and IMF say about unallocated gold?

Two institutions define the authoritative framework for understanding unallocated gold: the London Bullion Market Association and the International Monetary Fund. Their classifications are not academic. They shape how banks, central banks, and regulators treat these positions in practice.

The LBMA’s OTC guide establishes that unallocated accounts are the standard clearing mechanism for wholesale precious-metal markets. The LBMA states that a very large majority of interbank and OTC precious-metal trades clear over unallocated Loco London accounts. Operationally, this means unallocated is not a niche or retail-only product. It is the infrastructure of the professional gold market. The LBMA also makes clear that unallocated balances represent a contractual credit claim rather than title to specific bars, and that industry standards for allocated holdings require formal bailment, bar identification, and documented custody.

The IMF’s classification has practical investor implications beyond reserve accounting. If unallocated gold is treated as a deposit-like financial asset, it follows that account holders are creditors, not property owners. In insolvency, creditors recover through the claims process. Property owners recover their property. That legal distinction is the reason institutional investors with long-term mandates consistently prefer allocated structures for strategic reserves.

Key implications for investors:

  • Unallocated positions do not appear as “gold owned” in the strictest accounting sense; they appear as a financial claim.
  • Central banks that hold unallocated gold with commercial banks may need to reclassify those holdings in official reserve statistics.
  • In U.S. bankruptcy proceedings, an unallocated gold account holder’s claim would generally be treated as an unsecured debt, not a property right, unless the account agreement explicitly establishes a trust or bailment.
  • Allocated gold held under a proper bailment arrangement is generally treated as the client’s property and falls outside the provider’s bankruptcy estate.

The World Gold Council’s glossary reinforces this framing, defining unallocated gold as a claim on gold recorded as a balance with an intermediary, with counterparty risk as the defining characteristic. Galami’s perspective on unallocated gold in institutional portfolios

The conventional advice in most gold investment guides is to treat unallocated gold as inherently inferior to allocated, full stop. That framing misses how professional portfolios actually work. Unallocated accounts are not a compromise. They are a tool, and like any tool, the question is whether you are using the right one for the job.

At Galamigold, the approach for institutional clients is a hybrid structure: maintain a liquid unallocated tranche for operational needs, trading activity, and short-term collateral, and hold an allocated tranche for strategic reserves where physical delivery certainty and segregated custody are non-negotiable. The allocation between the two depends on the client’s mandate, liquidity requirements, and risk tolerance, not on a blanket preference for one model.

What most guides also understate is the audit discipline required to hold unallocated positions responsibly. Knowing your provider’s physical backing ratio, reviewing independent audit reports, and understanding the exact insolvency treatment in your account agreement are not optional due-diligence steps. They are the difference between a managed credit exposure and an unexamined one. Galamigold’s custody arrangements include regular independent audits and clear delivery guarantees, so clients holding unallocated positions know exactly what they are holding and what it would take to convert.

The risk is real. But so is the utility. The goal is to hold each type of gold in the right proportion for the right purpose, with full transparency on what each position actually represents.
