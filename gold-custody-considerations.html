Gold Custody Considerations: A Fiduciary’s Practical Guide

TL;DR:

  • U.S. fiduciaries should ensure gold is stored in a segregated, allocated, and IRS-approved depository with independent audits and proper insurance. Custody arrangements are critical for legal title, risk management, and tax compliance, with allocated and segregated storage offering the strongest protections. Proper documentation, audit coordination, and risk controls are essential to maintaining asset protection and regulatory compliance.

For U.S. investors and fiduciaries, the right approach to physical gold custody is segregated, allocated storage at an IRS-approved depository or licensed institutional vault that meets independent audit, insurance, and chain-of-custody standards. That single sentence covers the verdict. Everything below explains what it takes to implement it correctly and what happens when you cut corners.

Before going further, here are the non-negotiables every fiduciary must require:

  • Legal title proof: Written confirmation that specific bars or coins are assigned to the client, with serial numbers on record.
  • Allocated and segregated storage: The metal is physically yours, not a claim on a pool.
  • Independent audits: Third-party physical counts and reconciliations, at least annually, with the auditor’s name and report date in the contract.
  • Named insurance with stated limits: A certificate naming the client (or the custodian on the client’s behalf) with full replacement value coverage and no ambiguous sublimits.
  • Clear withdrawal and transfer procedures: Documented timelines, fees, and conditions for taking delivery or transferring title.
  • IRS-compliant custody structure for tax-advantaged accounts: Metals held through a qualified custodian and stored at an approved depository, never in personal possession.

A fiduciary checklist and implementation timeline follow in the sections below.

What does “custody” actually mean for physical gold?

The word “custody” covers several distinct arrangements, and the differences carry real legal and financial weight.

Allocated vs. unallocated storage

Allocated storage assigns specific bars or coins to a client. Each piece carries a serial number tied to that client’s account. The investor owns the metal outright. If the custodian or depository becomes insolvent, allocated assets sit outside the bankruptcy estate because they are not the depository’s property.

Unallocated storage works differently. The client holds a contractual claim on a pool of metal, not title to specific pieces. That claim is an unsecured liability of the custodian. In a default scenario, unallocated clients join the creditor queue, not the asset-recovery line.

Segregated vs. commingled storage

Segregated storage keeps a client’s metal physically separate from all other holdings, often in a dedicated vault section or cage. Commingled (pooled) storage mixes bars from multiple clients in a shared space, with ownership tracked by ledger rather than physical location.

Segregation matters most when you need to withdraw specific bars, verify serial numbers in person, or demonstrate clear title to a counterparty. Commingled allocated storage is still allocated (you own specific bars), but those bars sit alongside other clients’ metal, which can complicate physical audits and same-day withdrawals.

Custody Model Who Owns the Metal Insolvency Risk Typical Fee Level Best Fit
Allocated + Segregated Client (specific bars) Low — outside bankruptcy estate Highest Institutional, HNW, fiduciary accounts
Allocated + Commingled Client (specific bars, pooled location) Low — still outside estate Moderate HNW, mid-size accounts
Unallocated Custodian (client holds a claim) High — unsecured creditor Lowest Short-term trading, small accounts

Pro Tip: Commingled allocated storage is acceptable for accounts where specific-bar withdrawal is unlikely and cost efficiency matters. The moment a client needs to take physical delivery, demonstrate title to a lender, or satisfy a regulatory audit, segregated allocated storage is the only defensible choice.

What are the U.S. legal rules for gold IRA custody?

The IRS is unambiguous: Gold IRA precious metals must be held through a qualified custodian and stored at an approved depository. Personal possession of IRA-owned gold, regardless of how it is titled, constitutes a distribution and triggers taxes plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty for accounts under age 59½. The so-called “home storage Gold IRA” marketed by some promoters has no basis in IRS guidance and carries serious compliance risk.

Three distinct roles govern a properly structured Gold IRA, and keeping them separate reduces conflicts of interest and satisfies IRS requirements:

Role Who Fills It Regulatory Responsibilities Reporting Obligations
Custodian IRS-approved trust company or bank Administers the IRA, holds title on behalf of the account, files Form 5498 IRS Form 5498 (fair market value, contributions)
Depository Licensed vault facility Physically stores the metal, issues custody receipts, maintains chain-of-custody records Provides inventory statements to custodian
Dealer Precious metals broker Facilitates purchases, coordinates delivery to depository Issues trade confirmations and invoices

Commonly referenced IRS-approved depositories include Delaware Depository, Brink’s Global Services, and International Depository Services (IDS). Each operates under state and federal licensing requirements, carries independent insurance, and submits to periodic audits.

Verification steps fiduciaries should take: Confirm the custodian’s IRS-approved status, request the depository’s licensing documentation, and verify that the custodian files Form 5498 annually. For non-IRA accounts, the IRS approval requirement does not apply, but the same due-diligence standards for title, insurance, and audit remain best practice.

What security and audit controls should you demand from a depository?

Three control areas are non-negotiable: physical vault standards, personnel controls, and systems that maintain an unbroken chain of custody. Depositories that cannot document all three in writing should not be shortlisted.

Physical vault standards to require:

  • UL-rated vault construction (Class 3 or higher for institutional holdings)
  • Multi-factor biometric access with logged entry records
  • 24/7 monitored surveillance with off-site video retention
  • Tamper-evident seals on storage containers or bags
  • Serial-number tracking for every bar or coin, tied to the client’s account record

Personnel controls:

  • Background checks for all vault staff, with periodic re-screening
  • Dual-control access requirements (no single employee can access the vault alone)
  • Segregation of duties between vault operations, accounting, and client reporting

Systems and chain-of-custody:

  • Unique identifiers (serial numbers, weight, assay) recorded at intake and matched at every transfer
  • Documented transit manifests for any movement of metal between facilities
  • Reconciliation of physical inventory against ledger records at least quarterly

Audit protocol matters as much as the controls themselves. Independent physical audits and serial-numbered asset tracking materially lower counterparty risk for allocated holdings. Contractually, fiduciaries should specify audit frequency (annual minimum, semi-annual preferred), scope (full physical count, assay sampling on a percentage of bars, reconciliation against client statements), and the auditor’s qualifications. A Big Four accounting firm or a recognized precious metals auditor such as Bureau Veritas adds credibility that an in-house count does not.

Pro Tip: Require the depository to provide the auditor’s name, firm, and report date in every annual statement. A depository that conducts audits but refuses to name the auditor is not conducting audits you can rely on.

These controls translate directly into contract language. The custody agreement should warrant specific vault standards, name the auditor, set a reporting cadence, and define what happens if a discrepancy is found. Vague language like “industry-standard security” is not enforceable. Specific UL ratings, named auditors, and stated reconciliation timelines are.

What does custody insurance actually cover, and what should you watch for?

Fiduciaries should expect coverage that includes physical loss from theft, transit loss, employee dishonesty, and natural disaster. Major depositories combine comprehensive insurance with UL-rated vaults and independent audits; Delaware Depository, for example, carries coverage through Lloyd’s of London. That combination sets a reasonable benchmark for what institutional-grade custody looks like.

What the certificate says matters as much as the headline coverage amount. Common issues to check:

  • Named-perils vs. all-risk wording: All-risk policies cover any physical loss not explicitly excluded; named-perils policies cover only the events listed. All-risk is the stronger standard.
  • Sublimits: A policy with a $1 billion headline limit may cap transit losses at $10 million or employee theft at $5 million. Ask for the full schedule of sublimits.
  • Deductible layers: Some policies only pay after a large deductible, effectively leaving small losses uninsured.
  • Who is the named insured: The depository is typically the named insured. Confirm whether clients are named as additional insureds or loss payees, which matters in a claim scenario.

To verify coverage, request a current insurance certificate directly from the depository, not from a marketing brochure. Confirm the insurer’s AM Best rating (A- or better is the standard threshold). Ask whether the premium is pooled across all clients or passed through as a line item on your fee statement.

Pro Tip: Insist that your custodian agreement specifies primary coverage, not excess coverage that only triggers after other policies are exhausted. And confirm that the loss-payee structure protects the client’s interest directly, not just the depository’s balance sheet.

How does vault jurisdiction affect your legal rights as a U.S. investor?

Jurisdiction determines which courts hear disputes, which insolvency laws govern a depository failure, and which reporting obligations apply to U.S. taxpayers. For most fiduciaries, a domestic IRS-approved depository is the lowest-risk baseline: U.S. law applies, legal recourse is straightforward, and FBAR and FATCA reporting obligations are minimal.

Offshore private vaults can provide jurisdictional diversification and some protection against domestic systemic risk, but they introduce repatriation complexity, foreign legal frameworks, and additional U.S. tax reporting requirements. Popular offshore jurisdictions include Switzerland, Singapore, the Cayman Islands, and Liechtenstein. Each has different documentation standards, withdrawal mechanics, and legal protections for foreign investors.

The core tradeoff for U.S. fiduciaries: A domestic IRS-approved depository offers legal clarity, straightforward recourse, and no additional reporting burden. An offshore vault may reduce concentration risk in a systemic scenario but requires explicit repatriation planning, foreign legal counsel, and compliance with FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and FATCA (Form 8938) reporting. The diversification benefit is real; the operational and compliance cost is also real.

Jurisdictional diversification can protect against domestic political or systemic banking risk, but it requires explicit repatriation and legal-contest plans for U.S. fiduciaries. Without those plans documented in advance, offshore custody is a risk management gap, not a risk management tool.

Red flags for any offshore custody arrangement:

  • Opaque ownership structure of the vault operator
  • No independently audited financial statements
  • Unclear or undocumented withdrawal procedures
  • No U.S.-qualified legal counsel familiar with the jurisdiction
  • Inability to provide chain-of-custody documentation in English

Practical steps: before committing to any offshore arrangement, have U.S. tax counsel confirm the reporting obligations, obtain a written legal opinion on repatriation rights under the vault’s local law, and verify that the depository’s audit history is available and independently verified. For gold investment risks specific to institutional accounts, the jurisdictional layer is one of the highest-consequence variables.

How much does custody cost, and when does it stop making sense?

Custody costs include setup fees, handling charges, minimum account fees, transaction costs, and delivery charges, which can materially affect net returns, especially for small accounts.

To illustrate, smaller accounts often face higher relative custody expenses that may exceed returns from lower-cost gold investment options, while larger accounts benefit from economies of scale that make direct custody more cost-effective. Fiduciaries should carefully evaluate custody costs relative to account size and investment goals.

Fiduciaries managing smaller accounts should consider whether pooled gold exposure or an ETF structure better serves the client’s cost and liquidity needs before committing to direct custody. The governance obligation is to match the custody structure to the account’s economics, not to default to the most complex option.

How do you evaluate and select a custodian? A fiduciary checklist

Use a documented RFP process and a 10-point fiduciary checklist. Every item should be verified with written evidence, not verbal assurance.

The 10-point checklist:

  • Legal title: Obtain a written statement confirming allocated ownership with serial numbers assigned to the account.
  • Allocation confirmation: Verify that specific bars or coins are recorded in the client’s name in the depository’s ledger.
  • Audit history: Request the last two audit reports, including auditor name, firm, scope, and findings.
  • Insurance certificate: Obtain the current certificate of insurance, confirm the insurer’s AM Best rating, and review sublimits.
  • Reporting cadence: Confirm the frequency and format of account statements, and whether they include serial numbers and bar weights.
  • Fee schedule: Get a complete written fee schedule covering all charges, including minimums and delivery costs.
  • Withdrawal mechanics: Obtain written procedures for physical delivery, in-kind transfer, and liquidation, including timelines.
  • Jurisdiction and legal structure: Confirm the depository’s state of incorporation, licensing, and applicable insolvency law.
  • Counterparty exposures: Ask whether the custodian sub-custodies to a third party and, if so, who bears the counterparty risk.
  • Operational SLAs: Require documented service-level agreements for statement delivery, audit completion, and discrepancy resolution.

For evaluating gold traders and custodians, the same due-diligence framework applies: document every response, require evidence rather than representations, and treat any refusal to provide documentation as a disqualifying red flag.

Questions to ask on provider calls:

  1. Who conducts your physical audits, and can I receive a copy of the most recent report?
  2. Is my account allocated and segregated, or commingled with other clients’ metal?
  3. What is the process and timeline for physical delivery of my specific bars?
  4. Who is the named insured on your policy, and am I listed as a loss payee?

Red flags that should halt engagement:

  • Opaque or verbal-only fee schedules
  • Refusal to provide an insurance certificate or auditor’s name
  • Vague audit schedules (“we audit periodically”)
  • Any claim that home storage of IRA gold is permissible
  • Undisclosed sub-custodian relationships
  • No written withdrawal procedure

How do you open a custody account and keep it running correctly?

The typical timeline from custodian selection to fully operational custody runs six to twelve weeks, depending on account complexity, shipping logistics, and insurance sign-off.

  1. Select custodian and depository (Week 1–2): Complete the RFP process, review contracts, and execute the custodian agreement and depository storage agreement.
  2. KYC and compliance onboarding (Week 2–3): Submit entity documentation, beneficial ownership information, and AML/KYC materials. Custody account compliance at this stage mirrors other institutional asset classes: expect identity verification, source-of-funds documentation, and account purpose statements.
  3. Depository onboarding (Week 3–4): The depository opens the client’s storage account, assigns a client identifier, and confirms storage terms in writing.
  4. Funding and purchase (Week 4–6): Wire funds to the custodian, execute the purchase through the dealer, and confirm trade settlement.
  5. Physical delivery and assignment (Week 5–8): The dealer ships metal to the depository. The depository records serial numbers, issues custody receipts, and assigns bars to the client’s account.
  6. Initial audit and reconciliation (Week 8–12): Confirm the depository’s intake records match the dealer’s shipping manifest. Obtain the first custody statement with serial numbers, weights, and assay details.

Documents to obtain and retain:

  • Signed custodian agreement and depository storage agreement
  • Insurance certificate (current, with policy number)
  • Chain-of-custody receipts for every transfer
  • Assay and serial-number records for each bar or coin
  • Trade confirmations and dealer invoices
  • Audit reports (retain all historical reports)

For ongoing governance, risk management frameworks recommend documented SLAs, periodic testing, and clear responsibility matrices between the fiduciary, custodian, and depository. Reconcile the depository’s statement against the custodian’s records quarterly. Any discrepancy should trigger a written inquiry within five business days, with a documented resolution path.

Pro Tip: Assign a named internal contact at both the custodian and depository before the account opens. Escalation paths that exist only in an email thread are not escalation paths. Get them in the contract.

What are the tax implications of holding physical gold in custody?

Physical gold held in custody is classified by the IRS as a collectible, which carries a maximum long-term capital gains rate of 28% rather than the standard 20% rate that applies to most other long-term capital assets. That 8-percentage-point difference is material for high-net-worth investors and fiduciaries managing taxable accounts.

For Gold IRAs, the tax treatment depends on account type. Traditional Gold IRA distributions are taxed as ordinary income. Roth Gold IRA distributions are tax-free if the account meets the five-year holding and age requirements. In both cases, the IRS requires the custodian to report fair market value annually on Form 5498, which establishes the account’s value for required minimum distribution calculations.

Estate tax treatment follows standard rules: physical gold held in custody is included in the gross estate at fair market value on the date of death. There is no special exclusion for precious metals. Fiduciaries managing estate assets should confirm that custody agreements allow for title transfer to estate beneficiaries without triggering a forced liquidation, and that the depository’s procedures accommodate estate administration timelines.

For taxable accounts, cost basis tracking is the fiduciary’s responsibility. Each purchase creates a separate lot with its own acquisition date and cost. When bars are sold or delivered, the fiduciary must identify which lots are being disposed of (FIFO, specific identification, or average cost) and report accordingly. Custodians and depositories do not always provide lot-level cost basis tracking, so fiduciaries should maintain independent records from day one.

One practical note: storage fees paid on non-IRA accounts are generally not deductible as investment expenses under current tax law following the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, which suspended miscellaneous itemized deductions through 2025. Fiduciaries should confirm the current treatment with tax counsel for their clients’ specific situations.

This article provides general information, not tax or legal advice. Confirm the current rules with a qualified tax professional for your specific situation.

Why custody is a governance decision, not a logistics one

Most fiduciaries treat custody as an operational checkbox: pick a depository, sign the agreement, move on. That framing is wrong, and the consequences of getting it wrong show up years later, not at account opening.

Custody is where legal title, counterparty risk, insurance adequacy, and tax treatment all converge. A fiduciary who delegates those decisions to a dealer’s recommended depository without independent verification has not managed risk. They have transferred it to someone with a different set of incentives. The controls described in this article, from serial-number tracking to named-auditor requirements to loss-payee structures, exist precisely because the interests of the custodian, depository, and dealer do not automatically align with the interests of the client.

Long-term capital preservation in physical gold depends on the quality of the custody structure as much as the price of the metal. A well-documented, independently audited, fully insured custody arrangement is not overhead. It is the asset protection itself.

Authoritative sources to consult during custody due diligence

Every fiduciary conducting custody due diligence should work from primary sources, not marketing materials. The following resources are the starting points:

  • IRS Form 5498 guidance: The IRS’s own documentation on fair market value reporting for IRA accounts, including Gold IRAs. Confirms annual reporting obligations for custodians and what fiduciaries should expect to receive.
  • IRS Publication 590-B (Distributions from IRAs): Covers the rules on prohibited transactions, home storage restrictions, and distribution treatment for IRA-held precious metals. Available at irs.gov.
  • IRS-approved depository references: A working list of depositories referenced in IRA guidance, including Delaware Depository, Brink’s Global Services, and International Depository Services (IDS). Use as a starting point, then verify each depository’s current licensing status independently.
  • Depository custody agreements: Request the standard-form custody agreement from any depository under consideration before signing. Review for audit provisions, insurance warranties, withdrawal procedures, and liability caps.
  • Custody account compliance frameworks: Bank-focused guidance on KYC, AML, and custody agreement mechanics that translates directly to gold custody onboarding.
  • Galamigold institutional resources: Educational content, due-diligence checklists, and custody implementation guidance for institutional clients and wealth managers.

What to look for in each source: Confirm that the document is current (IRS publications are updated periodically), that the depository’s licensing is active in its state of operation, and that any insurance certificate you receive is dated within the last 12 months. Outdated documents are not evidence of current compliance.
