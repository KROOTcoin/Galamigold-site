Institutional Gold Broker Checklist: 2026 Vetting Guide

TL;DR:

  • Institutions should verify custody, insurance, and audit standards in writing before engaging a gold broker. Before you engage any physical gold broker, verify five things in writing: allocated and segregated custody with serial-number-level proof, named insurer with per-occurrence limits, an independent third-party audit schedule, a transparent trade tape with timestamped settlement pricing, and a documented SLA for delivery and repatriation.

Top priorities to request before any further discussion:

  • Custody model confirmation: allocated, segregated, with client-specific vault certificates and bar serial numbers
  • Insurer identity and per-occurrence policy limits (target at least $1 billion per facility, per occurrence)
  • Third-party audit schedule: annual minimum, with surprise visits permitted
  • Historical trade tape or execution proof with valuation timestamps
  • Written settlement SLA covering allocation, transport, and delivery windows

Pro Tip: Ask for the insurance certificate and a sample allocation manifest on day one. If either is withheld or vague, stop the conversation there. Brokers who handle institutional mandates have these documents ready.

What this checklist covers and what it intentionally excludes

This guide is written for U.S.-based institutional investors, wealth managers, financial institutions, and high-net-worth clients evaluating physical gold brokers for treasury allocation, portfolio integration, or high-value custody arrangements. It is not a retail dealer guide, a coin-collector checklist, or a consumer authentication how-to.

In scope:

  • Institutional treasury and portfolio allocation decisions
  • High-value physical gold transactions and custody arrangements
  • Broker and platform vetting for RFPs and onboarding
  • U.S. regulatory context: OFAC sanctions screening, FinCEN AML requirements, and IRS reporting obligations

Explicitly excluded:

  • Retail dealer selection or consumer gold-buying advice
  • Numismatic or collectible coin authentication
  • Retail-focused pricing comparison or scam-avoidance tips

The U.S. jurisdiction focus matters because AML/KYC expectations under the Bank Secrecy Act, OFAC sanctions obligations, and tax treatment under IRS collectibles rules differ materially from other markets. A broker structured for European or offshore clients may not meet U.S. compliance standards without specific documentation.

The full gold broker checklist: documents and pass/fail thresholds

Each item below maps to a specific document or verifiable evidence type. Request all of them before signing any mandate.

Custody model

Confirm allocated, segregated storage with bar-level serial numbers assigned to your account. Pooled or unallocated arrangements expose you to counterparty insolvency risk. Acceptable proof: a client-specific vault certificate listing bar serial numbers, weight, and purity, issued by the vault operator.

Vault security standards

Professional vaults should hold UL 608 Class 3-rated doors and panels, which withstand 120 minutes of sustained attack using power tools and cutting torches. Request the vault’s certification documentation and a recent third-party site audit report. Third-party controlled access, armed security, and alarm redundancy are baseline expectations, not differentiators.

Insurance and transport

Per-occurrence insurance structured at substantial coverage per facility, per occurrence, underwritten by Lloyd’s of London syndicates, is the institutional standard. An aggregate annual cap is structurally weaker because a single large loss can exhaust coverage for the rest of the year. Request the full policy certificate and confirm the per-occurrence vs. aggregate wording in writing.

Auditability and reporting

Tri-party oversight — vault operator, investment platform, and independent auditor — is best practice. Annual audits are a minimum; surprise reconciliation visits are a strong trust signal. Request sample audit reports and ask whether unannounced audits are contractually permitted.

Pricing transparency

Require spread and markup disclosure, spot reference timestamps for each trade, and a historical trade tape showing execution prices. Settlement FX policy should be documented if cross-currency transactions are involved. Opaque pricing or refusal to provide execution proof is a disqualifying condition.

Liquidity and sourcing

London Good Delivery bars remain the dominant institutional format: 62% of central bank respondents selected them as their top purchase option, and 93% identified them as the primary way to hold gold reserves. Confirm the broker sources from LBMA Good Delivery refiners or equivalent verified mints, and request refiner documentation. Buyback and liquidation commitments should be in writing with example settlement timelines.

Counterparty and balance-sheet strength

Request audited financial statements, any regulator filings, and credit lines supporting custody obligations. Vault operators and logistics firms that file audited financials provide more verifiable counterparty information than those that do not. Confirm counterparty exposure limits and custodial agreement terms.

Compliance and controls

Request AML/KYC policy documentation, OFAC and sanctions screening procedures, SAR reporting policy, and remediation workflows. For more on U.S. AML obligations in gold trading, the regulatory stakes are significant. Sample compliance policy excerpts are a reasonable ask during RFP.

Operational documentation

Request a sample sale and purchase agreement or brokerage agreement, the settlement and delivery SLA, chain-of-custody paperwork, and dispute resolution clauses. These documents reveal how the broker actually operates, not just how they describe themselves.

How to run operational and legal due diligence

Turn the checklist above into an ordered workflow assigned across legal, operations, and compliance teams.

  1. Request primary documents. Insurance certificates, most recent audit report, sample allocation manifest with bar serial numbers, and the full fee schedule. Set a 5-business-day response deadline.
  2. Validate insurer and vault credentials. Contact the named insurer directly to confirm the policy is active and the per-occurrence wording is accurate. Verify the vault’s UL 608 Class 3 certification and Licensed Depository status where applicable.
  3. Run compliance checks. Review the AML program documentation, run sample KYC checks, and confirm which OFAC lists the broker screens against and at what frequency.
  4. Financial and counterparty review. Obtain audited financial statements and any available credit opinions. Confirm the balance sheet can support custody obligations for your intended allocation size.
  5. Operational test. Execute a small initial allocation, then audit the chain-of-custody documentation and reconcile the settlement against the trade tape. Operational execution quality — documentation accuracy, settlement timing, and allocation confirmation — determines whether institutions treat gold as a true treasury instrument.
  6. Contract negotiation. Lock in SLAs, audit access rights, indemnity provisions, and per-occurrence insurance wording before signing. These terms are far easier to negotiate before the relationship starts.

Pro Tip: During step 5, request the chain-of-custody paperwork for the test allocation before you ask for it on the real trade. How quickly and completely it arrives tells you more than any sales presentation.

How custody and execution choices affect your portfolio

Allocated, segregated custody means the gold is legally yours, identified by serial number, and sits outside the broker’s balance sheet. In an insolvency, it is not available to creditors. Unallocated or pooled arrangements are a creditor claim against the broker, not a property right. For institutional balance-sheet treatment, that distinction drives asset recognition, audit trail requirements, and risk committee documentation.

Execution models and their operational implications:

  • Spot physical delivery: fastest liquidity, but requires vault readiness and transport logistics to be pre-arranged
  • Allocated vault-to-vault transfers: lower transport risk, longer settlement window, requires dual-vault documentation
  • Buyback/liquidation: confirm the broker’s committed bid-ask spread and turnaround time in writing before you need it

Central banks are increasingly diversifying storage locations, moving beyond sole reliance on London vaulting toward domestic and multi-jurisdiction arrangements. For U.S. corporate treasuries or sovereign-adjacent funds, multi-jurisdiction vaulting reduces single-point-of-failure risk and supports repatriation planning.

Custody/Execution Model Best Fit
Allocated, segregated domestic vault Long-term reserve, balance-sheet asset
Allocated, multi-jurisdiction vault Sovereign/corporate treasury, geopolitical hedging
Pooled/unallocated Short-term tactical liquidity only — not recommended for core allocation
Spot physical delivery Active trading programs with pre-arranged logistics

Pro Tip: For multi-jurisdiction vaulting, require a repatriation protocol document before committing. It should specify transport insurer, customs documentation, and timeline. Brokers who cannot produce this are not operationally ready for sovereign or large corporate mandates.

What onboarding and settlement timelines actually look like

Institutional onboarding typically runs 3–6 weeks when both sides are prepared. Legal negotiation on the brokerage agreement is usually the longest variable.

Milestone Typical Days to Complete
KYC/AML document submission Day 1–5

Settlement for a standard physical gold trade runs T+2 for execution to allocation confirmation, with international transport adding 3–10 business days depending on origin and destination. Ownership proof issuance should follow within 24–48 hours of vault receipt.

On fees, expect transaction spreads in the range of 0.1%–0.5% for institutional lot sizes, storage fees typically quoted in basis points annually, and insurance either bundled or passed through as a line item. Minimum ticket sizes for institutional programs generally start at $500,000 equivalent.

Pro Tip: Negotiate a fee cap for the first 12 months, including a ceiling on insurance pass-through increases. Brokers who agree to this are signaling confidence in their cost structure. Those who resist often have variable cost exposure they have not fully priced.

Red flags that should end the conversation

Some omissions are not negotiable. Stop engagement immediately if you encounter any of the following:

  • No per-occurrence insurance or refusal to name the underwriter in writing. An aggregate cap or unnamed insurer is not institutional-grade coverage.
  • No independent third-party audits or refusal to permit unannounced reconciliations. Self-reported inventory is not auditable.
  • Unallocated storage with evidence of rehypothecation or lending without full written disclosure. Your gold should not be working for someone else’s balance sheet.
  • Opaque pricing, no historical trade tape, or refusal to provide execution proof. Pricing opacity is where margin extraction hides.
  • No UL 608 Class 3 vault certification for significant-value storage, or refusal to provide a sample SLA or contract. These are baseline operational documents, not proprietary information.

What the standard checklist misses

Most gold broker checklists stop at custody and insurance. What they underweight is the operational test: a small, real trade executed before the full mandate is signed. The chain-of-custody paperwork from that test trade tells you more about a broker’s actual processes than any compliance questionnaire. A broker who handles institutional volume has these workflows running daily. If the documentation on a $50,000 test allocation is slow, incomplete, or inconsistent, the documentation on a $50 million allocation will be worse under pressure, not better.

The other underweighted factor is fee structure transparency over time. Transaction spreads are easy to compare. Storage fee escalation clauses, insurance pass-through variability, and termination costs are where the real cost divergence appears at 24 and 36 months. Negotiate caps on all three before signing, not after the relationship is established.
